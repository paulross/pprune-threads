Perhaps it follows that Annex 13 investigations aren't the 'be-all-and-end-all' of accident investigations? Are you able to identify any error in the French prosecutor's investigation or how it did damage to anyone that should not have been done?
No, however:

While I have not studied France's law applicable to aviation accidents generally or investigations in particular, it is my understanding that French law does allow earlier, or more direct, participation - or both - in aircraft accident investigations. Annex 13, on the other hand, does not contemplate determination of blame or liability which are substantially the same thing as sought to be determined by prosecutors (albeit in a different form of court proceeding).

Also, one of the most fundamental principles, if not the most fundamental principle, underwriting all of what ICAO does - at least within its traditional or historic remit and not drifitng off into DEI or strident environmental causes - from the Convention through the Annexes and SARPs, is standardization. Obviously criminal investigations will not be, and could not become, standardized across the States which are active in the international civil aviation sector.

At the time of the Alaska Airlines door plug incident and its aftermath, long-time aviation policy worthies decried the way in which the status of the NTSB investigation had been dragged into the criminal matter against Boeing pending in federal district court in Texas. Their reasoning was that accident investigators must have access as unfettered as possible to sources of information, and the presence of criminal processes necessarily makes people reluctant to speak or provide information otherwise. With this reasoning in mind, it could be agreed that although Annex 13 and pertinent SARPs are not the ultimate in investigatory enlightenment, criminal inquiries (and the French version of same) are not an inherently better approach.

Of course, when reasonably specific and articulable facts point to potential criminal conduct, that is a very different matter.

I'd suggest some consideration about the use of certain terms in reference to the actions being attributed to the pilot who is said to have turned the fuel control switches off.

Though without cataloguing everything in the current thread (and the earlier ones), one widely held view is that a pilot moved the switches as a suicidal act. Another widely held view is that the pilot moved them volitionally but without suicidal intent (mental or physical impairment or impulse largely described as the trigger). Some posters and outside sources point to having only a preliminary report and are unconvinced some system or hardware fault is not the root cause. Some few others don't speak to cause but instead criticize the conduct of various commentators, on the forum and elsewhere.

I've noted this because my suggestion, while it applies most directly to the first group, also applies to the second, and in likely fewer instances to the other two sets of views as well.

"Murder" is a crime defined in the substantive criminal law of each jurisdiction. Generally speaking it requires, as a form of the "mens rea" - criminally culpable mental state required for a crime to have been committed in the eyes of the law - "malice aforethought" for the (classically defined) "Murder One" or Murder in the First Degree. Such malice generally consists of prior deliberation and planning. Second degree murder does not require such prior deliberation and planning but still requires intent to kill the person or persons.

Then there are lesser gradations of unlawful homicide, such as voluntary manslaughter, involuntary manslaughter, and more - of course depending on the form of legal development and articulation of statutory and common law in any given jurisdiction.

I certainly realize that most if not all posters who have used the terms "mass murder", or "suicide/mass murder", or just "murder" in reference to the actions some view as the pilot having taken are using the terms outside of any actual legal discussion. More than once in 12 years on this forum I have been told to take my legal or legalistic comments over to the "bar association forum" where they may belong and might be welcome. So this suggestion isn't about trying to turn an aviators' discussion, in which some others also participate, into a law forum.

But using the terms "mass murder", "suicide/mass murder", and/or "murder" is ascribing an actual motive to kill the other crew members and passengers, to the pilot who the given poster believes, or who the poster says others believe, moved the switches. I think this is unfair, perhaps even improper. Some level of homicidal responsibility, yes. if the switches were moved. To some likelihood, a form of manslaughter (I'm not a practitioner of criminal law, however, so - caveat noted). But murder, in the legal term's meaning, means an intent to kill all the other people. We do not know this to be the case. As a profesional even if not an aviation professional as such, I suggest it is better aligned with the purposes and history of this forum not to ascribe, to the deceased (professional) pilot, such ultimate serious criminal intent. Responsibility is not the same thing as this level of criminal intent.

One big caveat caveat to this is that I have not dug into all the case histories of the identified instances of suicide by pilots operating airliners, let along instances where it was suspected but not established (and heavens no, not MH 3-7-something). If adjudications turn up from those prior instances where, somehow, under the law applicable in some jurisdiction, murderous intent within the eyes of the law was determined to have existed, I stand to be corrected.

I also explicitly acknowledge that few posters, if anyone at all, has intended to use the terms I have referenced in their legal meaning. But as the progress of the investigation moves forward from the Prelim Rpt, and as the litigation machines rev up, I beliveve this suggestion is properly made now. And better to make the suggestion now, if and when the CVR transcript is leaked before the AAIB's final report.
